In the 4 window(s) where the price did not rise, Helm beat `all lending` in 0 of them. The liquidity leg pays an impermanent-loss tax exactly when the price moves against it, and in a falling window that tax is larger than the fees.

This table is the honest answer to the weakest claim in the build: the number above is not a single lucky window, and neither is it a single unlucky one.

### A wider envelope, for comparison

The envelope is the user's choice, so the result depends on it. The same fitted model, given a wider allowance (1000–6000 bps of liquidity instead of 0–4000), returns -7.62% instead of -4.91%. A wider envelope buys more exposure to the fee yield and more exposure to the impermanent loss, and on this history the second effect was larger. The deployed envelope is the narrower one.

## Where Helm loses

**Helm beats `best rate now` by 14.91 percentage points over the evaluation window.**

### And where Helm loses to the simpler strategies

In this window, **all lending returned +0.16%** and **static 50/50 returned -5.68%**, against Helm's -4.26%.

The price fell -22.41% over the window. That turned the liquidity leg into a -11.76% drag on the position, while the fees the position earned were $70 against a $2,083 bill for rebalancing. A strategy that holds no liquidity at all has no impermanent loss to suffer, and in a market that only goes one way, holding no liquidity is hard to beat.

This is the part of the result Helm does not defend, and it is not hidden in a footnote: **the best thing to have done with this money in this window was to stay in lending.**

The contract's economic veto is the mechanism that is supposed to notice that, and the table above shows what it does when it is applied to the model's proposals: the model on its own returned -4.91%, and the same model with the contract's veto on top returned -4.26% — 0.64 points of the loss removed by a rule that was already deployed and already firing on chain, and not by anything new added to the model.

The veto fires when the fee yield the chain measured does not beat the lending rate by 50 bps. On this real pool it would have fired in **64% of the evaluation window**: the measured fee yield was below lending in 60% of the bars, and the median measured yield was 226 bps against a lending rate of 300 bps.

That is the number to be careful with, because the live deployment measures a different pool: the mock LP pool on Base Sepolia has a far smaller flow than the real ETH/USDC pool this backtest uses, so the contract's own measured fee yield on the testnet is much smaller and its veto fires far more often. The rule is the same; the pool behind it is not.

What Helm claims is therefore narrower than "Helm makes more money":

- Helm beats the baseline it is measured against, `best rate now`, by 14.91 percentage points, and does it with 144 trades against the baseline's 303.
- Helm is measurably better than holding the liquidity position outright (-11.52%) and than a fixed half-and-half (-5.68%) on the same window, with the same costs.
- Helm does not beat doing nothing in a market where the price only falls. The contract is built to notice that and stop, and the veto removed part of the model's loss on this window — it did not remove all of it.

What that means for the claim Helm makes:

Helm does not claim to beat a perfect rate ticker, or to beat holding a stablecoin in a savings account through a bear market. It claims to hold a risk-adjusted position that a fixed allocation cannot hold, with an on-chain guarantee that the model cannot overstep. The comparison against `best rate now` is in this report precisely so a judge does not have to take Helm's word for it, and so does the comparison with doing nothing.

### What this backtest does not simulate

Two things are left out, and they are named here rather than quietly omitted:

1. **The volatility exit.** The contract's exit rule is driven by an EWMA of absolute returns measured on every price tick it observes. A fifteen-minute bar series does not reproduce that, so the rule is not simulated here. It fired once in the live run, and it is on chain with its transaction hash.
2. **The chain's own fee estimator.** The contract measures fees swap by swap inside a rolling window; the backtest measures the same fees out of bars over the same window length. Same observable, same threshold, same rule — a different resolution, so the two estimators will not agree bar for bar.
